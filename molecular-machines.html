A scientific paper can say that a molecule enters a membrane. It is harder to picture what meets, turns, and makes room. The video starts with peptoid 1, a twelve-residue, protein-like helix below a negatively charged DPPG lipid monolayer.1 Gold marks its water-avoiding aromatic side chains; blue marks its positively charged ones. Each lipid has a polar head and two tails.

Three stages to watch

  1. Attract. The charged face approaches the lipid heads. The approach illustrates a plausible electrostatic attraction; its path was not recorded.
  2. Engage. Charged groups meet the surface as the helix reorients. The neighboring lipid heads and tails in the mechanical view match those in the molecular view.
  3. Insert. Part of the peptoid passes through the headgroup region toward the tails. X-ray reflectivity supports partial insertion; a roughly 56° tilt is inferred only if the helix stays intact.2

The sweeping divider reveals the same parts in two representations. That mapping turns a static density profile into a question readers can inspect: which groups face water, which meet the heads, and which reach the tails? The exact contact sequence and motion are a teaching model, not measured footage.

How the agent skill works

This was made with Anatomy, an agent skill: instructions, drawing tools, examples, and checks that a coding agent follows. Give the agent a subject, evidence, and the interaction to explain. It builds SVG parts, projects them into a consistent view, ties motion to a small model, and checks the result in a browser.3 Here, both views share geometry and move together. Anatomy guides the illustration; it does not calculate molecular dynamics.
