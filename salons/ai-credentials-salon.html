The Unbundled PhD

Here's the pre-read for our salon, "How do credentials shift in science in the age of AI?", hosted with the BioKEA team and Claude Community.

Everything in here comes from the readings sent in ahead of the dinner. Footnotes are at the bottom, each with a short summary of the piece.

It's about a 14-minute read.

TL;DR

  • The doctorate is about 800 years old, and the research PhD about 200. Today it packages knowledge, hands-on lab skill, access to equipment and experts, and a stamp that says "trust this person."
  • The bundle was shaky before AI showed up. Only 49–64% of US doctoral students finish within 10 years, roughly 1 in 8 engineering PhDs can get an academic job, and 51% of STEM PhD programs lose money once you count the people who don't finish.
  • AI is unbundling it. A software engineer used ChatGPT and AlphaFold to design a cancer vaccine for his dog. A math student built a fusor in his bedroom in four weeks with Claude. A model now beats 80th-percentile PhD experts at troubleshooting unpublished lab protocols.
  • So far, AI has taken the cheap parts. The dog still needed a sequencing center, an RNA institute and someone else's ethics approval. The fusor made plasma, not fusion. The scarce parts are picking the question and proving the answer.

Part 1

The Story of Humans and Credentials

Let's zoom way out.

For most of history, if you wanted to know whether someone could do something, you watched them do it. A medieval apprentice became a journeyman, and a journeyman became a master by submitting one big piece of work to the guild. That's literally where the word "masterpiece" comes from. The credential and the skill were the same object, and you could pick it up and look at it.

Then knowledge got too big to look at. Nobody can watch you do science the way you can inspect a chair. So we invented a proxy: spend years near people who already know, produce one original piece of work, and they'll vouch for you.

The doctorate itself is medieval. At universities like Bologna and Paris in the 1200s, a doctor's degree was basically a license to teach. The research PhD, the version where you have to discover something new, is much younger. It comes from the University of Berlin, founded in 1810, whose philosophy faculty started requiring a dissertation that added something new. Americans who studied in Germany brought it home, and in 1861 Yale handed out the first three American PhDs: one in philosophy, one in physics, one in classics.1 Over the next century, the degree went from optional extra to the price of admission. Today a PhD is the default requirement to be a professor, a research scientist or the person whose name goes at the end of the author list.

Think of a credential as compressed trust. Instead of checking someone's work yourself, you check who checked it. That's a great deal when checking is expensive.

But the deal has gotten worse for the people buying it. Here's what the PhD looks like from the inside today:

That last bar matters for our story. The credential isn't just "PhD." It's "PhD from where, with whom," and 80% of US faculty were trained at 20% of universities.4 The stamp has a brand on it.

The money is also awkward. Tommy Blanchard, who left neuroscience for industry data science, says his undergrad computer science degree did more for his career than his PhD.5 One analysis of graduate programs found that once you account for the years of lost salary and the people who drop out, 51% of STEM PhD programs have a negative return on investment, up from 32% if you only count people who finish.6 And a Swedish study of more than 20,000 PhD students found psychiatric medication use matched their peers before the PhD started, then rose about 40% by year five.7

To be fair to the PhD, Blanchard is still glad he did his. He got to watch his advisor shrug at a confusing result and say the paper could just be wrong, and ask "how big is the effect size?" when an undergrad brought in a headline.5 That habit of mind is hard to get anywhere else. Hold onto it, because it comes back later.

So that's the situation as of a few years ago: an expensive, slow, brand-sensitive credential that a lot of people still found worth it. Then things got weird.

Part 2

The Weird Stories

Story one: the bedroom fusor. In August 2024, Hudhayfa Nazoordeen, a University of Waterloo math student with zero hardware experience, posted that he'd built a nuclear fusor in his bedroom in a couple of weeks. His secret, he said, was Claude 3.5 Sonnet plus Projects. He loaded the datasheets for every component into Claude and used it to learn the parts and debug his vacuum system. Total: about four weeks and $2,000.8

Blue Box: What is a fusor?

A fusor is a small vacuum chamber with a wire cage in the middle at very high voltage. Fill it with a little gas, crank up the voltage, and ions get flung toward the center hard enough to glow as plasma. If the gas is deuterium and everything is tuned just right, a few nuclei actually fuse, and you can prove it by detecting the neutrons that fly out.

Amateurs have built these for decades. The hobbyist forum keeps a "Neutron Club" for people who proved fusion. A 2008 Wall Street Journal story counted 42 members.9

Here's the detail that gets lost in the headline: Hudhayfa got plasma, but by the coverage's account, no neutrons.8 AI got him to a glowing chamber in a month. It didn't get him into the Neutron Club. Remember this one too.

Story two: Rosie. In 2024, Paul Conyngham, a Sydney tech entrepreneur with an engineering background and no medical training, found out his dog Rosie had cancer. Chemo and surgery didn't stop it. So he asked ChatGPT, which suggested immunotherapy and pointed him to UNSW's Ramaciotti Centre for Genomics. He paid them to sequence Rosie and her tumor, then used AlphaFold to look for mutated proteins worth targeting. Pall Thordarson's UNSW RNA Institute turned his data into a bespoke mRNA vaccine in under two months. Rosie got her first shot in December 2025 and a booster in February, and most of her tumors shrank dramatically.10

Thordarson called it the first personalized cancer vaccine designed for a dog. An AI startup CEO called it a sign the world is about to get very weird. Conyngham went on to found a startup, Gamgee, which reports a seed round led by Founders Fund and a dog trial with UNSW, the University of Queensland and the Garvan Institute.10

Story three: the math. This fall, the frontier labs started dropping solutions to famous open problems. Álvaro Lozano-Robledo, writing on Terence Tao's blog, reports that OpenAI spent roughly $15 million in compute to solve Navier-Stokes (he amends that to "scoop"), and that its October release attacked about 4,000 open problems and made progress on about 700.11

Put these three stories together and you get the question students are actually emailing their professors right now. Lozano-Robledo opens his post with one: an undergrad who dreamed of math academia, now asking whether there's any point, and saying they'd been lost for months. He lists the questions he keeps getting. Will AI surpass every human researcher? Will researchers become professional prompters? Will only the brightest minds get to contribute? And the big one: should I do a PhD at all?11

Phillip Isola at MIT got enough versions of the same email from AI PhD students that he wrote up ten of them, with answers.12

If an engineer with ChatGPT can design a cancer vaccine for his dog, what exactly is the six-year degree for?

Part 3

Unbundling the PhD

The trick is to stop treating the PhD as one thing. It's a bundle, like cable TV. When you sign up, you get:

  1. Knowledge. The vocabulary, history and methods of a field.
  2. Tacit skill. The stuff nobody writes down: which step in a protocol always fails, what a bad gel looks like.
  3. Access. Equipment, compute, reagents, and world experts down the hall.
  4. The stamp. Permission. Your name on a grant, an ethics application, a paper.
  5. Judgment. The "that paper could be wrong" reflex, plus knowing which question is worth five years.

Cable got unbundled when streaming made each channel cheap to get on its own. So which channels is AI streaming?

Knowledge is going first. This is the obvious one. Lozano-Robledo says he now uses LLMs every day to finally understand techniques he'd always had questions about, and that his students can learn faster because the model is available 24/7, versus his one or two office hours a week.11 Conyngham didn't need an immunology degree to learn what a neoantigen was.

Tacit skill is going faster than anyone expected. A Stanford PhD student shared this in notes for the salon: lots of wet-lab knowledge is tacit and not written down anywhere a model could train on.13 That used to be the moat. Then OpenAI built TroubleshootingBench. PhD scientists wrote up 52 protocols they'd personally run, changed them enough that they appear nowhere online, and wrote three failure scenarios each, every one caused by a subtle mistake.14

Blue Box: What is tacit knowledge?

Tacit knowledge is knowing how without being able to fully say how. You can read a recipe for bread, but the baker knows by feel when the dough is ready. In a lab, it's the postdoc who knows the centrifuge in room 204 runs hot, or that this antibody only works if you skip the second wash.

It matters here because tacit knowledge is the main thing you can only get by standing next to an expert. That's a big part of why the PhD is an apprenticeship and not a course.

The Stanford student's response is still worth hearing: even if the models are catching up on tacit knowledge, being in a lab with the world experts on your topic saves you months or years of reinventing things that have already been optimized.13 A benchmark measures whether you can diagnose a failure. It doesn't hand you the person who already knows which of six failures is happening in your hood.

Access is not going anywhere. Look at Rosie's story again, but this time list who did what. ChatGPT made the plan. AlphaFold ranked the targets. But a university genomics center did the sequencing. A university RNA institute made the vaccine. And it was given under ethical approval at the University of Queensland, after a reportedly 100-page ethics application that took three months. It was also given alongside a checkpoint inhibitor, a standard immunotherapy drug, so nobody can say how much was the vaccine. A UNSW researcher put it plainly: this was not a clinical trial.10

The credential didn't disappear from Rosie's story. It moved. It stopped living in the person with the idea and started living in the institutions that could act on it. Conyngham borrowed everyone else's stamps. (Later coverage reports that Rosie's cancer eventually spread and she was put down in June. The vaccine bought her time, which is what Conyngham said it would do.)10

Compute is a kind of access too, and here academia is in a weird spot. One estimate floating around puts a whole ML PhD at 5,000 to 30,000 GPU-hours, which is plausible arithmetic but not measured data.15 In a 2024 survey of 50 researchers at 35 institutions, the typical setup was 4 GPUs for days at a time, and 85% had zero cloud budget.16 For scale:

The biggest frontier run in that chart used at least a thousand whole PhDs' worth of compute, even at the high end of that estimate. If the credential ever meant "this person has access to the best tools in the field," that stopped being true in AI a while ago.

Which leaves the last two channels: the stamp, and judgment. Those are the ones that are hard to stream.

Part 4

The Convex Hull

Lozano-Robledo has the best mental model I've seen for what AI is doing to research. He borrows it from Nestor Guillen, who calls it a toy model.11

Imagine every idea in mathematics as a point in a huge space. The ideas at the frontier stick out like spikes: discrete geometry over here, algebraic number theory way over there. Now stretch a rubber sheet over the tips of all the spikes. Everything inside that sheet is the convex hull: every result you can reach by combining ideas that already exist.

An LLM's sheet is a little rounder than that. It fills the space between the spikes beautifully, but it smooths off the sharpest tips, the newest and least-written-about ideas at the very edge of a field, because that's where its training data is thinnest.

The toy model says LLMs, trained on everything humans have written, are spectacular at filling in the hull. Lozano-Robledo's example is the AI disproof of Erdős's unit-distance conjecture: it looks brilliant because it joins two far-apart spikes, but it sits inside the hull. He quotes the mathematician Melanie Matchett Wood saying, in effect, that the right human team putting in similar hours would have found it.11

If that's right, two things follow. AI progress should be explosive now, while there's lots of empty hull to fill, and then slow down, until someone pokes a new spike outside. And poking new spikes is the human job.

The best comment on the post pushes back hard. A self-described mid-career mathematician calls the hull idea "very misleading," because most mathematicians spend their whole careers inside the hull, and a genuinely new spike comes along once in a generation, if at all.11 Translation: if the job of the median PhD was filling in the hull, then the median PhD is exactly what's being automated. Lozano-Robledo's optimism may be true for the field and still not much comfort for a student.

The Stanford student's notes land in a similar place from the biology side. Problems with clear paths to execution and validation, like designing a drug against a known target or knocking down every gene in a cell type and measuring what happens, look very amenable to AI. Projects where the first challenge is figuring out what question to ask look much less so, and that's where a lot of curiosity-driven academic research starts.13

That's why Stanford's Michael Fischbach is so interesting here. His 2024 Cell paper argues that scientists put far too little effort into choosing problems compared with solving them, and he opens with a line that should be on every lab wall: scientists "spend days choosing a problem and years solving it."18 Anthropic turned his course into a Claude skill: nine steps, from generating ideas and stress-testing their assumptions to building decision trees with go/no-go points for when experiments fail. For a grad student, the suggested timeline is two to four weeks of thinking before you commit.19

Tiny aside: yes, the tool for teaching humans to pick problems AI can't solve is itself an AI. Make of that what you will.

Part 5

The Validation Wall

Here's the other thing AI hasn't touched much: finding out whether you're right.

The Stanford student's sharpest point is that the main bottleneck in creating knowledge is still validating the hypotheses and mechanisms that computational analysis generates, and that validation still takes months to years because measurement and perturbation technology is limited.13 In their view, that's what still makes grad school worth doing right now. Their example: AlphaFold predicts a protein's static structure, but in a real cell proteins are constantly folding and refolding. We've barely scratched the surface of what we can see.

Notice what this does to the job. If AI throws ten times more hypotheses at the wall, the person who can get one through the gap becomes more valuable, not less. Lozano-Robledo reports exactly that on the math side: the number of research projects he's involved in tripled in a few months, and he's recruiting more student collaborators than ever to test which ideas actually go anywhere.11

Isola makes a quieter version of the same point. The rate-limiting step in becoming an expert isn't the technology, he says, it's the human brain and our social systems. If it took you N hours to become an expert before, it'll take about N hours in the future, maybe a bit less.12 He also passes along a phrase from someone else that's worth stealing: expertise is safety infrastructure. When machines produce results nobody can fully check, you want humans around who understand each piece.

Remember the fusor? This is the same lesson. Plasma is the hypothesis. Neutrons are the validation. The Neutron Club exists because the hobbyists decided long ago that a glowing chamber isn't proof of anything.9

Part 6

How PhD Students Are Using AI

So what does a PhD student actually do differently in 2026? A Stanford PhD shared this field report from their rotations. Here are the top five.13

  1. Learn how to learn. More than ever, it's the main skill. And knowing a field's vocabulary and history makes you a much better prompter, which is why graduate classes still matter.
  2. Go hybrid wet/dry. It's now feasible, and maybe soon expected, to be a rigorous bench scientist and a computational one, with agents running analyses while you're at the bench.
  3. Read at industrial scale. In one rotation, they had Claude read about 50 papers on every gene in an unexplained bacterial gene region and write up what the region might do, using Paperclip (an AI-friendly view of the literature, including figures and supplements) inside Claude Code's dynamic workflows.2021
  4. Keep a living record. Mycelium, from Arjun Raj's lab at Penn, automatically logs decisions and findings as you analyze, because most analytical work otherwise disappears.22
  5. Pick the problem before you start. The Fischbach problem-selection skill from Part 4 walks you through go/no-go checkpoints before you commit years to a question.19

What hasn't changed: validation still takes months to years, which in their view is why grad school is still worth it, at least for now.

Part 7

So, What Should a Credential Certify?

For 200 years, the PhD has certified one main thing: this person can do the work. They can read the literature, run the protocol, do the analysis and write the paper, and a group of experts watched them do it.

That certificate is getting cheaper by the month, because the work is getting cheaper. An engineer can plan a vaccine. A math student can build a plasma chamber. A model can troubleshoot a protocol better than most PhDs.

But look at what was missing in each weird story. Rosie needed institutions to sequence, manufacture and approve. The fusor needed neutrons. The math needed humans to work out whether 700 results contain any new ideas at all. In none of them was the scarce part doing the work.

The people we read land in different places. Blanchard asks whether, even if a PhD doesn't lead where you imagine, you'd still want those years to be part of your life story.5 Isola says do it "for the love of the game."12 Lozano-Robledo says the decision should be the same one you'd have made a year ago.11 His mid-career commenter thinks the field is about to shrink.

Some food for thought before we sit down:

  1. If the work keeps getting cheaper, what is a credential actually vouching for: skill, judgment, access, or just that someone stuck it out for six years?
  2. Rosie's story borrowed every stamp from institutions. Is trust moving from people to labs, or will new kinds of stamps appear?
  3. If most scientific careers happen inside the hull, what is the typical scientist for once AI fills it in?
  4. Can taste in questions be taught, measured or certified? By whom?
  5. When a model designs the experiment and a robot runs it, who is accountable for the result?
  6. If you were 22 and loved science, what would you do with the next six years?

Discussion Guide

Three threads for the room. Tap a theme to see its questions.

1. What does a PhD actually certify, and is it still the right signal?
  1. What did your PhD experience teach you that couldn't have been learned any other way?
  2. What skills matter most in research today that a PhD doesn't reliably signal?
  3. If AI can do much of the literature review, analysis, and writing, how should a PhD evolve and adapt?
  4. What would a credible alternative path into frontier research look like? Who's already doing it?
  5. If you were hiring for a research team tomorrow, what would you look for instead of, or alongside, the degree?
2. What does a good human-AI loop look like in the lab?
  1. Describe a moment when AI meaningfully sped up or changed your research. What made it work?
  2. Where should the human stay in the loop, and where are we holding on out of habit or fear?
  3. How do you know when to trust an AI-generated hypothesis, analysis, or result?
  4. What's a failure mode you've seen or worry about, such as quiet errors, overconfidence, or lost intuition?
  5. What tool or interface do you wish existed to make the loop better?
3. How does the role of the scientist evolve when experiments run more autonomously?
  1. If a self-driving lab runs 1,000 experiments overnight, what's left for the scientist to do in the morning?
  2. Does the core skill shift from doing experiments to choosing the questions? How do we train for taste?
  3. Who gets credit for a discovery made mostly by an autonomous system?
  4. What might we lose, such as serendipity, hands-on intuition, or apprenticeship, and does it matter?
  5. Ten years from now, what does a day in the life of a great scientist look like?
