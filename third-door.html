Stop Applying, Start Apprenticing

How to create a map of projects to make yourself relevant for your top role.

This is about a different way to go after a job you want: start apprenticing yourself to it before you apply. I call the plan for that a Shadow Apprentice Map.

It's about a 12-minute read. The prompt is in Part 4 if you just want to make one.

TL;DR

  • The front door is crowded. Polishing a résumé and reading about the role is what everyone in line is doing.
  • There's always a third door. Alex Banayan's idea: skip the line and find the side entrance. For a job, one way in is doing a small version of the work before anyone asks you to, which AI now makes possible in a day.
  • A Shadow Apprentice Map is that plan, written down. Five levels, from 10 hours of fundamentals to a decade-long moonshot, and every card has to fit the role, the company and you.
  • One prompt draws it. Behind it, four research agents worked through about 560,000 tokens in under five minutes and boiled them down to 30 cards with 83 sources.
  • It's never sunk cost. Even if you don't get the job, you keep the skills, the public work and the people who saw it.

Part 1

The Front Door

Here's how most people apply for a job they really want.

They find the posting. They read it four times. They rewrite their résumé so the words "cross-functional" appear in it. They read the company blog, maybe watch a podcast with the CEO at 1.5x speed. They hit submit, refresh their inbox for two weeks, and if they get an interview, they spend the night before memorizing talking points about why they're "passionate about the mission."

Nothing about this is wrong. It's just what you're told to do. And that's the problem: it's exactly what you're told to do, so it's exactly what everyone else is doing too.

Picture the line outside a popular club. Everyone has the same outfit on. Everyone is holding the same résumé. The bouncer has a few seconds per person, so he's mostly looking for reasons to say no.

If you're lucky, you're already famous and you walk through the VIP entrance. Most of us aren't. So we wait in line and hope.

Part 2

The Third Door

Alex Banayan spent years as a college student trying to interview people like Bill Gates and Lady Gaga, which is not the kind of thing you get by standing in line. What he found is that a lot of them got started the same way. They didn't wait at the front door, and they didn't have the VIP pass. They went around the side.1

His version: there's always a third door. You jump out of line, run down the alley, knock on the kitchen door a hundred times, climb through a window. It's harder to find, and almost nobody else is using it. He tells the whole story in his talk at Google.

How do we make that interesting idea actionable in the age of AI?

Start with how people used to get jobs. For most of history, nobody applied to be a blacksmith. You hung around a blacksmith, swept up, held things, and slowly got trusted with hotter metal. In England, the law set a minimum: seven years bound to a master before you could practice a trade on your own.2 By the time anyone called it a job, you'd been doing it for years. Apprenticeship was the front door.

Modern hiring swapped that for a document and an interview. That's efficient, but it means most people now walk into a job having never done any of it, and the person hiring them has to guess.

The hard part of apprenticing yourself was always the master. Someone had to tell you what was worth learning and check whether you'd done it right. That's the part AI just made cheap. An agent can read a company's docs, blog and job posting in minutes, tell you what the team actually builds, and help you build a small version of it by tonight.

So here's the side entrance: start doing the job before you have it. Not pretending you have it. Doing a small, honest, public version of it. Ship the demo the team would ship. Write the memo they'd write. Build the eval they'd want. Then show up with that.

This does two things at once, and the second one is sneaky.

First, it's a try-out for you. You find out whether you actually like the work before you've uprooted your life for it. A lot of dream jobs are dream jobs mostly from the outside.

Second, it makes luck. The entrepreneur Jason Roberts has a nice formula for this: your "luck surface area" is how much you do times how many people you tell.3 Call it build times tell.

Momentum compounds, too. The first demo is hard. The second one borrows half its code from the first. By the third, you're talking about the role in the past tense: "When I built X, I found Y."

You can't always find a third door. But you can build one: do a piece of the job, in public, before anyone gives it to you.

Which raises a practical question: which version of the job? For which company? Starting where? That's what the map is for.

Part 3

Meet the Shadow Apprentice

A shadow apprentice brings the old way back, without asking permission. You pick the role. You apprentice yourself to it in the open: learn what that team has to know, then build what that team would build. Nobody's supervising you, so the agent becomes your first teacher and real users become your second. That's the "shadow" part: you're shadowing a job nobody gave you.

Blue Box: What's a Shadow Apprentice Map?

A one-page plan that turns three inputs (a job description, a person and a company) into five levels of work.

Levels 1 and 2 are a 24-hour speedrun: the fundamentals, then three small demos. Level 3 is one real project with real users, over a few weeks. Levels 4 and 5 are the long arc: a startup-sized product and a decade-sized moonshot.

The rule that makes it work: every card has to be relevant to the role, to the company and to the person. Miss one and it's cut.

Why bother with Levels 4 and 5 if the job is Level 1? Because the interview is partly a test of how big you think, and "here's the decade-long project I'd point your product at" is a much better answer to "where do you see yourself?" than "in a senior role."

Part 4

The Prompt

I built the first one for a friend I'll call Connor. Here's the prompt. Give your agent the job description and the person's profile (LinkedIn, portfolio, writing) first, then paste this.

The prompt
Given the role, and given [name] is interested in [their focus], make me a "shadow apprentice map."

Level 1: fundamentals they should know, from technical (SDK, API) to foundational (ethics, the company's constitution or values).
Level 2: initial projects to ship, and why.
Level 3: one robust project that gets real user feedback.
Level 4: a product that could be a startup.
Level 5: an agent that could win a Nobel Prize.

Every card must be relevant to the role, relevant to the company, and relevant to the person. Before writing, do deep research on the company and its recent work, and save it as reference files. Make this legit: help them speedrun the role in 24 hours, with great projects to demo.

Visual notes:
- Make it an interactive HTML page.
- Put levels across and tracks down, so each skill is its own card.
- Cards open one at a time, with the detail front and center.
- Every card cites its sources with links I can click.
- Light mode only.
- Replace the person's real name with a made-up one.

Part 5

How the Map Gets Made

The prompt is about 180 words. What it sets off is a lot bigger, and the gap between the two is the whole trick.

Step one: read everything, fast. The agent split the research four ways and ran it in parallel: one agent on Anthropic's mission, safety work and constitution, one on the developer docs, one on Anthropic's AI-for-science work, and one on everything Connor had ever published. Between them they made 134 tool calls and worked through about 562,000 tokens, roughly five novels' worth, in under five minutes.4

Then they threw almost all of it away. Five novels became 12,883 words of reference notes, about 3% of what was read. That compression is where the judgment happens.

Why not just ask the model what it knows? Because the facts that mattered most were the newest. The fact the whole map hinges on, Anthropic's $150M commitment to the Department of Energy's Genesis Mission, was four days old when the map was built.5 It wasn't in any model's training data. A map built from memory would have pointed Connor at last year's company.

Step two: make every card earn its place. Each of the 30 cards has to name the job-description line it proves, the company product or bet it connects to, and the part of Connor's history it builds on. That's 90 checks. Anything that could sit on anyone's list gets cut, which is how you avoid ending up with "Learn AI in 30 Days" with a logo on it.

Step three: find the gap, then aim everything at it. Good research turns up an argument, not just facts. For Connor it was this: every public Anthropic science case study was in biology or pharma, while its newest bets (the Model Hardware Standard, the Claude Science workbench and the Genesis commitment) point at the physical world.675 Nobody had publicly shown Claude doing materials or hardware well. Connor is a materials and mechanical engineer. So all 30 cards point at that hole.

From first prompt to published map took under an hour of my time. The agents did the reading. I did the edits.

A map should make one argument. If yours reads like a list of things to learn, the research hasn't found the gap yet. Ask the agent: "What is this company betting on that nobody has shown working yet, and why is this person suited to it?"

Part 6

Connor's Map

Three insights set up the whole map, one for each input:

  • The role: Applied AI Associates are Anthropic's builders on customer engagements: agents, skills, evals and demos, plus discovery calls.
  • The person: Connor is a materials and mechanical engineer who already rolled Claude out at a hydrogen startup, and he runs an algae bioreactor at home.
  • The company: Anthropic's public science wins are all in biology, but its newest bets are in the physical world.

Put those together and the projects almost write themselves. Here are three, from a day to a decade:

  1. 2

    Instrument agent · one day

    Claude runs his bioreactor through an MCP server, with hard safety limits enforced in code. The demo ends with Claude refusing an out-of-limits command on camera and explaining why. It proves the job's "demo environments" line using Anthropic's newest hardware bet and a device only he owns.

  2. 3

    Design-review agent · 4 to 8 weeks

    An agent that reviews real parts for 5 to 10 small hardware teams before they hit expensive testing. Every miss becomes an eval case. It ends with a product-feedback memo to Anthropic, which is literally a line in the job description.

  3. 5

    The Nobel agent · a decade

    Seven agents and one human scientist hunting for an iridium-free catalyst for green hydrogen. Iridium is one of the rarest metals on Earth, and the property that matters can be measured by a machine, so an agent can check its own work. A skeptical reviewer agent blocks any result that hasn't been reproduced.

That last one handed Connor his opening line. It's now the first thing on the map:

"Anthropic has shown Claude designing proteins. I want to help it discover a material, and I'd start with the iridium bottleneck in green hydrogen, because there verification can be automated and the payoff is decarbonizing heavy industry."

Compare that to "I'm passionate about the mission." One of these people is standing in line.

Part 7

Even If You Don't Get the Job

Here's the part I like most. A résumé you tailored for one company is worth nothing if they say no. A map isn't like that.

Every card you finish is a skill you keep. Every demo you ship stays public, keeps collecting views and keeps doing its build-times-tell math long after the job closes. The people who saw it still saw it. And the next map you draw, for the next role, starts at Level 2 instead of zero, because half the fundamentals carry over. It's not sunk cost. It's compound interest on competence.

Which brings up the real tension in this whole idea.

Connor's map has 30 cards. With agents doing the reading and the scaffolding, you could rush through first drafts of all 30 in a day. You could also spend two years on them. Level 5 could take a lifetime. All three are true, and the trick is knowing which speed each card needs.

Agents are fast at the left side of that line: reading a field, drafting, scaffolding, checking. They can't speed up the right side. Real users take weeks to trust you. Taste comes from shipping things and watching them break. A real discovery takes as long as the world takes to answer.

So don't pick one speed. Use agent speed to get to the starting line today, and spend your human hours where only a human can earn them.

For most of history, trying out a job meant years of your life. Right now, for the first time, you can try at something close to the speed of agents. That's worth a shot. Pick a role. Draw the map tonight. Ship Level 2 this weekend.

Worst case, you end the week more capable, with three public projects and a sharper sense of what you want. Best case, someone on the inside sees the work before they ever see your résumé.

Before you draw your map, ask:

  1. What is this company betting on that nobody has shown working yet?
  2. What's the strangest thing in my background that fits that bet? (Connor's was a bioreactor.)
  3. Which of my Level 2 demos could a team member forward to their boss tomorrow?
  4. Who am I going to tell, and where?
