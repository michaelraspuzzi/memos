# A restaurant cannot buy a Michelin star, refuse one, or appeal losing one. Louisiana pays $350,000 a year for the inspectors to come.

**Founder audience: builders of ratings where the rated party is not the customer. The Michelin star is the most valuable award in commercial hospitality and the only one whose recipients have no relationship with the issuer at all — they do not apply, cannot pay, cannot withdraw, and have no right to an explanation. That is precisely what makes it worth something, and it is also why Michelin has spent a century looking for someone else to bill. It has now found two: destination marketing organisations, which pay six and seven figures for inspectors to visit their territory, and travellers, who book hotels through Michelin's own platform after Michelin rates them. The first model is the cleanest solution ever devised to the ratings-funding problem. The second reintroduces the exact conflict the first was built to avoid.**

## The position in five facts

- **The guide has never been a business on its own terms.** André and Édouard Michelin gave away 35,000 copies in 1900 to make people drive further and wear out tires; stars for individual restaurants began in 1926 with 46 recipients, and the one-two-three system arrived in 1931.[^1] Michelin's 2025 group results were €26.0 billion of sales, €2.9 billion of segment operating income and €1.7 billion of net income — and the guide is not a reported segment, so its standalone economics have never been published.[^2]
- **Destinations now pay for coverage, and the amounts are public where records laws apply.** Michelin's American South guide is funded by $1.65 million a year over three years: $350,000 each from Louisiana, North Carolina, South Carolina and Tennessee, and $125,000 each from Mississippi and Alabama.[^3] Texas committed $2.7 million over three years across the state travel office and five city bureaus; Boston's bureau signed a three-year deal reported at just over $1 million; Minneapolis pays $250,000 a year and Indianapolis and Milwaukee $150,000 each.[^4]
- **A state can decline, and one did.** Virginia turned down $120,000 a year for three years — $360,000 — and stayed out of the South guide.[^5]
- **Under those agreements, the tourism boards submitted restaurant recommendations.** Records obtained by Axios through a Louisiana public records request show the marketing agencies fed candidate restaurants into the process that determines which establishments get inspected.[^3]
- **The rated party has neither exit nor appeal.** Sébastien Bras asked to be left out of the 2018 guide after a decade at three stars; Michelin re-listed Le Suquet at two stars in 2019 regardless. Marc Veyrat sued to make Michelin disclose why his third star was removed and lost in January 2020.[^6]

## What Michelin actually sells

For most of the guide's life the answer was tires, indirectly, and copies of a red book, directly. Neither pays for what the guide costs. Michelin employs full-time salaried inspectors who eat roughly 300 restaurant meals a year each and, in the hotel business, pay full public rates and fly on Michelin's money.[^7] The company has never confirmed how many inspectors it has; outside estimates cluster at 150 to 200, and Michelin's refusal to say is itself part of the product.[^8]

The value of the star to a restaurant is a demand shock the restaurant did not purchase. The cost of the star to that restaurant is a permanently raised fixed-cost base — staffing ratios, produce, china, linen, the kitchen brigade required to hold the standard through a Tuesday in February. This is the asymmetry that makes the award frightening to receive as well as to lose, and it is the reason chefs periodically ask to be removed. They cannot be. Bras's request in 2017 was granted for one edition and then reversed by Michelin's own decision in 2019.[^6]

The Veyrat judgment is the other half. A French court declined to require Michelin to disclose its inspection reasoning; the guide owes the rated party no explanation.[^6] Put the two together and the structure is exact: **participation is involuntary, the criteria are unreviewable, and the recipient has no standing.** Every property that would make this outrageous in a regulatory context is what makes the rating credible in a commercial one. Nobody suspects a Michelin star of having been negotiated, because there is no counterparty to negotiate with.

The corollary is unavoidable. If the rated party cannot pay, someone else must, and the identity of that someone is the entire design problem of the ratings business.

## The destination is the right buyer, for one specific reason

Visit California paid roughly $600,000 in 2019 for the first US state guide, and Michelin's guide director told the *New York Times* at the time that the money offset the cost of the reviews.[^9] The model spread. By 2025 and 2026, most new American coverage was contingent on a tourism board writing a cheque, funded in the usual way: Boston's share comes out of a hotel-room surcharge in Boston and Cambridge that raises about $35 million a year for tourism promotion; Minneapolis's comes from a 2% charge on hotel revenue expected to raise about $7 million, of which the Michelin payment is roughly 3.5%.[^4]

Why does this work where restaurant payments would not? Because a destination marketing organisation's interest is in *coverage*, not in *outcome*. Meet Minneapolis wants inspectors in Minneapolis; it has no stake in whether the star lands at one restaurant or another, and no ability to benefit if it lands wrongly. A restaurant paying for consideration has an interest in exactly one outcome. The two payments look similar on a bank statement and are structurally opposite.

That is the design insight worth stealing, and it generalises: **find the party whose interest is that the rating exist and be trusted, rather than that it come out a particular way.** In credentials, that is the licensing board rather than the candidate. In [chess](chess-elo.md), it is the federation buying rated status for its whole tournament rather than the player buying points.

Michelin's own version of the model has one visible crack, and Axios found it. If tourism boards nominate restaurants for inspection, the buyer has acquired influence over the sampling frame, which is not the same as influence over the verdict but is not nothing either.[^3] Michelin's position is that fees fund coverage and marketing and that a star is awarded for what is on the plate.[^10] That claim is now doing considerably more work than it did in 2018, and Michelin only confirmed the payments publicly in October 2025.[^7]

## Two customers, two very different contracts

**Boston, buying a launch event.** More than $1 million over three years produced 26 listed restaurants and exactly one star, awarded to an omakase counter in a South End basement.[^4] Read as a purchase of stars, that is a catastrophic price. Read as a purchase of a national press cycle, a permanent entry in a global travel database, and a reason for a food-motivated traveller to book two hotel nights, it may be perfectly rational — the hotel surcharge funding it exists precisely to generate hotel nights. The important point for a founder is that the buyer's success metric and the rating's success metric are different documents. Boston measures room-nights. Michelin measures whether anyone doubts the star.

**Virginia, buying nothing.** The state declined at $360,000, and subsequent reporting indicated Michelin's own assessment of the market's maturity was a factor in the terms.[^5] Virginia is the most useful data point in the entire model, because it isolates the variable. If coverage arrives when a state pays and does not arrive when a state declines, then coverage is a purchased good — and the map of Michelin-starred America is, to a meaningful extent, a map of which tourism budgets had room.

The comparator here is The World's 50 Best Restaurants, which funds itself through sponsorship and an academy of thousands of voting members. That model is transparent about being an opinion poll and is routinely accused of lobbying and campaign behaviour, because voters can be reached. Michelin's inspectors cannot be reached, which is the only asset the guide actually owns.

## The hotel move is the risk

On October 8, 2025, at the Musée des Arts Décoratifs in Paris, Michelin awarded its first global Key selection: 2,457 hotels — 1,742 with one Key, 572 with two, 143 with three — drawn from more than 7,000 hotels in the guide. Michelin's announcement states that all of them are "bookable via The MICHELIN Guide's website and mobile apps, with integrated concierge services and VIP perks."[^11]

Michelin has been able to do this since it quietly bought Tablet Hotels in 2018 and began selling stays directly on guide.michelin.com.[^12] So the hotel business has a structure the restaurant business never had: Michelin rates the property, and then earns when a reader books the property it rated. *Michelin has not published its commission terms, and the widely cited 10–15% range is an outside estimate rather than a disclosure; the structural point does not depend on the number.*

This is the conflict the restaurant guide was built to exclude, reintroduced on the other side of the transaction. A restaurant cannot pay Michelin for a star. A hotel can, in effect, pay Michelin every time a Key persuades someone to book — not for the award, but as a consequence of it. Michelin's answer, disclosed in the same October 2025 round of transparency, is that its inspectors pay full public rates and it covers their travel.[^7] That answers the *inspection* question. It does not answer the *selection* question: whether a hotel that is bookable through Michelin's platform is more likely to be evaluated than one that is not.

For a founder building a ratings product, this is the fork in the road stated as clearly as it ever gets. Michelin has run one hundred years of the pure model in restaurants and about seven years of the transactional model in hotels. The pure model produces an award nobody questions and revenue Michelin has never been able to disclose. The transactional model produces revenue that scales with the rating's influence and a question the company will now have to answer forever.

## Founder playbook

1. **Identify the party whose interest is that the rating be trusted, not that it come out a certain way, and sell to them.** Destinations, licensing boards, tournament organisers and insurers qualify. The rated party never does. *Test:* for each prospective customer, write the sentence describing what they gain if your rating is wrong in their favour. If that sentence exists, they cannot be your customer.
2. **Make participation involuntary and keep it that way.** Michelin's power comes from rating restaurants that never asked. *Test:* can a subject remove themselves from your index? If yes, your coverage is a negotiation and your scores are censored by the unhappy.
3. **Refuse to explain individual decisions, and be prepared to defend that in court.** Veyrat lost because a ratings issuer that must justify every verdict is a ratings issuer that can be litigated into consistency, which is the same thing as being managed by its subjects.[^6] *Test:* if your ten angriest subjects each demanded your reasoning, would your process survive publication? If it would not, fix the process; if it would, still do not publish it.
4. **Guard the sampling frame more carefully than the verdict.** Whoever decides what gets inspected has most of the power that matters, and Michelin let its paying customers into that step.[^3] *Test:* what fraction of your evaluated population was nominated by someone who paid you? Publish that number before a journalist derives it from a records request.
5. **Price coverage by territory, not by subject.** A per-territory contract has a natural buyer with aligned incentives, a multi-year term, and no per-verdict pressure. *Test:* could you set your entire price list without knowing a single subject's identity? If not, you are selling access.
6. **Think three times before you also own the transaction.** Michelin waited a century to become a booking channel and did it in hotels, not restaurants — a domain where the standard was newer and less load-bearing. *Test:* if you take a cut of the transaction your rating causes, can an outsider verify that non-bookable subjects are evaluated at the same rate? If they cannot, you have taken on a permanent, unfalsifiable accusation.

The durable rule: **an award is worth exactly what its recipient could not have obtained by paying for it.** Everything else in a ratings business — the fee schedule, the territory contracts, the booking platform — has to be constructed around preserving that one fact, because it is the only asset on the balance sheet that a competitor cannot replicate with money. Michelin has monetised its guide by selling to jurisdictions and to travellers, and has never once sold to a chef. The day that changes, the €26 billion tire company will still be fine, and the star will not.
