# A machine holds a top-200 rating on the best measure of human problem-solving ever built. The humans who still beat it are being paid by the hour to train it.

**Founder audience: builders of any score that claims to measure what a person can do. Codeforces is the finest capability rating in existence — Elo-derived, free, adversarially tested by 1.7 million people across more than a thousand contests, and impossible to buy. In December 2024 OpenAI reported that o3 scored 2,727 on it, roughly the 175th-best human alive. In September 2025 an OpenAI system solved all twelve problems at the ICPC World Finals and Gemini 2.5 Deep Think solved ten, both cracking a problem that none of the 139 university teams solved. Codeforces responded with an unenforceable honour rule. Employers responded by making hiring expensive again. And the same elite population that the rating identified was repriced — not as engineers, but as hourly suppliers of reasoning data to the labs whose models had just matched their scores. The generalisable finding: a rating that certifies *membership in a population* survives automation, and a rating that certifies *possession of an ability* does not.**

## The position in five facts

- **Codeforces is the purest ratings public good on the internet, and captures nothing.** Roughly 1.69 million registered users, more than 1,000 rated rounds, and an average of about 11,000 registrants per round, on a Elo-derived scale with published tiers — free to enter, free to read, with no title, licence or certificate attached to any number.[^1]
- **A model reached the top 200 of that scale in two years.** OpenAI reported a Codeforces rating of 2,727 for o3 in December 2024, against 1,891 for o1 three months earlier — a jump of 836 points, and past the 2,400 threshold that marks roughly the 99th percentile of contest participants.[^2]
- **Then it happened in public, under supervision.** At the ICPC World Finals in Baku on September 4, 2025, Gemini 2.5 Deep Think solved 10 of 12 problems in a combined 677 minutes, which would have ranked second overall against 139 university teams, of which only the top four took gold. An OpenAI system solved 12 of 12. Both solved Problem C, which no human team solved.[^3]
- **The issuer's only available defence was a rule it cannot enforce.** After o1, Codeforces prohibited AI-generated solutions in rated rounds, permitting translation and ordinary autocomplete but not generated logic. There is no proctoring; the integrity of a 1.7-million-person rating scale now rests on self-report.[^4]
- **Employers stopped trusting the cheap signal and bought labour instead.** Google reintroduced at least one in-person interview round — Sundar Pichai said on the *Lex Fridman Podcast* in June 2025 that the company wanted to "make sure" candidates had the fundamentals — and the shift back to face-to-face screening spread across the industry through 2025.[^5]

## Codeforces sells nothing, and that is the whole problem

Mike Mirzayanov built Codeforces at Saratov State University as a contest platform. It runs short rounds on original problems, scores by penalty time, and updates ratings on a system in the Elo/Glicko family. It is free, it carries advertising and sponsorship rather than subscriptions, and it issues no credential of any kind. There is no Codeforces certificate, no Codeforces title an employer must recognise, and nothing a holder can be denied.

For fifteen years this was a feature. The rating's credibility came from the fact that nobody could buy it and the issuer had no commercial interest in any individual score. Value accrued entirely to third parties who took it for free: recruiters at trading firms and large technology companies used a Codeforces rating as a costless pre-filter, and AI labs used the same scale as a benchmark. Neither ever paid Codeforces a cent for the privilege.

That is fine while the third parties agree on what the number means. It is fatal when they stop, because the issuer has no lever. Codeforces cannot reprice, cannot restrict access, cannot certify a subset of participants as verified, and cannot revoke anything from anyone, because it never granted anything. When a model posted 2,727, Codeforces had exactly one move available — write a rule asking people not to cheat — and it made it.

Contrast the structure described in [chess ratings](chess-elo.md). FIDE gives its rating away too, and waived €2.2 million of fees to grow the graph. But FIDE sells the *title*, and a title is the thing it can refuse. When Deep Blue beat Kasparov in 1997, and when engines passed every human permanently thereafter, not one FIDE rating point lost value and not one grandmaster title was questioned. The reason is exact and worth stating slowly: **a FIDE rating was never a claim that you were the best problem-solver on earth. It was a claim about your results against the other humans in the room, attached to a licence for life.** Engines are not in the room, and cannot hold the title.

A Codeforces rating is a claim about capability. Capability turned out to be reproducible.

## Two markets repriced in opposite directions

**The employer, who was buying a free filter and now has to buy verification.** Automated technical screening was one of the great cost-collapse stories of the 2010s. A take-home assessment or a scored coding round costs the employer roughly nothing per candidate, scales infinitely, and — this was the part everyone believed — correlated well enough with on-the-job ability. Every element of that depended on the candidate being unassisted.

Three vendor responses are visible in the market, and they are the three available answers to any authentication problem. Instrument the session and infer from telemetry, which scales cheaply and inherits a false-positive problem that falls hardest on honest candidates. Add environmental proctoring, which raises the cost and the intrusion together. Or put a trained human in the loop, live or reviewing against a rubric, which works and costs what humans cost. Google's return to an in-person round is the largest employer in the market choosing the third answer at the most expensive point in the funnel.[^5]

The consequence for a founder is a genuine market inversion: **for the first time since the assessment industry began, the price per verified signal is rising.** The business is no longer in generating scores. It is in attesting that a score belonged to a person.

**The lab, which is buying the humans who still beat the model.** While the rating's screening value fell, the market value of the population it identified rose, through an entirely different door. Expert-data marketplaces route vetted specialists — competitive programmers among them — to frontier labs to produce reasoning traces, problem sets and graded solutions. Mercor is the clearest case: reported at a roughly $10 billion valuation in 2025, with a network of more than 30,000 vetted experts, average pay reported in the $85–95 an hour range and daily payouts reported in the millions. *These are secondary and company-stated figures for a private company; the headline "annualised revenue" widely quoted is gross payment volume through the marketplace rather than net revenue, and should be discounted accordingly.*[^6]

Note the shape of the trade. The rating stopped functioning as a filter for a career and started functioning as a sourcing filter for an hourly contract. A 2,400-rated competitor in 2019 got interviews at firms that would employ them for a decade. A 2,400-rated competitor in 2026 gets a high hourly rate for producing the training data that makes the next model's 2,400 unnecessary. Both are real income. Only one of them compounds.

## What changed, in one table

| | Before (through 2023) | After (2025–26) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What the rating certified | Rare human problem-solving capability | The same capability, now also held by two commercial models |
| Who consumed it for free | Recruiters, as a pre-filter | Labs, as a benchmark |
| Cost per screened candidate to an employer | Near zero, automated | Rising; in-person and human-reviewed rounds returning[^5] |
| The issuer's enforcement options | None needed | None available; an honour rule[^4] |
| Where the population's income comes from | Full-time engineering roles | Also hourly expert-data contracts[^6] |
| Best-known model result | — | 2,727 Codeforces (Dec 2024); 12/12 and 10/12 at ICPC World Finals (Sept 2025)[^2][^3] |

The comparison that should worry any ratings founder is the second row. The single largest consumer of the Codeforces scale is now the set of organisations building the systems that saturate it. A benchmark's users become its adversaries the moment the benchmark is worth optimising against, and a free rating has no mechanism to distinguish the two.

## Did it work, and what would settle it

Codeforces itself is fine on every metric it can observe: rounds still run, registration still grows, the community is intact. That is the trap. Participation is a lagging indicator of a rating's external value, because people compete for reasons — enjoyment, rivalry, habit — that survive the collapse of the signal's market price by years.

Three things would settle the question, and none is visible yet:

1. **The 2026 ICPC World Finals in Dubai**, the 50th, scheduled for late 2026. The 2025 AI participation was an ICPC-supervised exhibition track scored alongside the human contest. Whether 2026 keeps that separation, merges it, or drops it will show how the sport's own governing body has decided to define its population.[^3]
2. **Whether any large employer states publicly that it still weights a competitive-programming rating in screening.** Nobody has, in either direction, which is itself informative.
3. **Whether Codeforces ever issues something it can refuse** — a verified-identity round, a proctored championship, a certificate. That would be the FIDE move, made under duress, twenty years late.

The decisive undisclosed number is what fraction of top-quartile Codeforces participants now derive meaningful income from expert-data work rather than employment. The marketplaces have the data and do not publish it, and it is the difference between "the rating found a new buyer" and "the rating's holders became contractors."

## Founder playbook

1. **Decide whether your score certifies a population or a capability, and write it down.** Population ratings — you beat these humans, in this competition, under these rules — survive machines, because machines are not in the population. Capability ratings do not. *Test:* rewrite your rating's definition without using the words "can" or "able." If you cannot, you are selling capability, and you should assume a model holds your top score within thirty-six months.
2. **Attach something you can refuse, before you need it.** FIDE has a title; Codeforces has nothing. *Test:* name the artifact a user would still hold if every number in your database were deleted tonight. If there is no such artifact, you have a leaderboard, not a credential.
3. **Never let your largest consumer be a party with an incentive to saturate you.** Free access to labs made Codeforces the default coding benchmark, which is exactly why it got optimised against. *Test:* list your top five consumers by volume and mark the ones who benefit from a high score appearing on your scale. If that list is most of your traffic, your scale is a target.
4. **Build verification as a product before your score needs defending.** The market is now paying for attestation, not assessment. *Test:* what would you charge, today, to certify that a specific score was produced by a specific identified human under observed conditions? If you have no answer, a competitor with worse content and better proctoring will take your customers.
5. **Watch where your population's income comes from, not where your traffic comes from.** Codeforces' engagement metrics did not move when the rating's hiring value fell; its users' pay structure did. *Test:* survey your top decile annually on how they earn. That is your leading indicator, and it is cheap.
6. **When the shock arrives, expect the value to migrate rather than vanish — and check whether the destination is durable.** The elite competitive-programming population is being paid more per hour than ever, on contracts with no tenure. *Test:* if your users' new buyer disappeared in a year, would they be better or worse off than before your rating existed?

The durable rule: **a rating survives automation only if it certifies who you competed against, not what you are able to do.** Ability is reproducible and gets cheaper every year; membership in a bounded population of humans does not. The chess federation, which looks like the least modern institution in this collection, happens to have got this structurally right in 1970 and has never had to change it. Codeforces built the better measurement and owns nothing it can defend — a distinction that also runs through [bouldering grades](bouldering-grades.md), where the rating only became a measurement once the artifact was fixed, and [esports rankings](esports-rankings.md), where a career converts into nothing the player can carry.
