# The chess rating that means something has 420,000 holders. The one that means nothing has 252 million accounts.

**Founder audience: builders of ratings, credentials and two-sided marketplaces. FIDE owns a real monopoly — the only chess number that converts into a permanent, portable title — and earns about nine percent of its income from it, while spending five years deliberately giving it away. Chess.com owns a rating that no federation, employer or tournament recognises, and used it to reach 252 million registered accounts and 2.6 billion games a quarter. Both are behaving correctly. In each business the rating is the loss leader: FIDE sells the licence the rating qualifies you for, chess.com sells the opponent the rating finds you. The recurring founder error in ratings products is to charge for the number, which is the one component with no defensible price.**

## The position in five facts

- **The rating monopoly is nine percent of the business.** FIDE's audited 2024 accounts report total income of €15,721,534. Every fee charged to national federations combined — membership, entry, registered (rated) tournaments and title applications — came to €1,474,290. Net income from FIDE's own events was €12,506,778, or eighty percent of the total.[^1]
- **FIDE is deliberately shrinking that nine percent.** It suspended rating fees on over-the-board rapid and blitz events in 2021, rated 59,603 of them for free through 2024, waived an estimated €2.2 million in fees, and extended the waiver through 2026.[^2]
- **Where it does charge, it charges cents.** Standard tournaments are rated at €0.30 per game, rapid and blitz at €0.10 per game before the waiver, Swiss events at €1 per player; a player with no FIDE ID triggers a €60 licence.[^3]
- **The rating that confers nothing is six hundred times larger.** Chess.com passed 252 million registered members by March 31, 2026, added 14.9 million registrations in the first quarter alone (up 23.8% year on year), averaged 9.7 million daily active users, ran 2.6 billion games in the quarter, and did it with 668 staff.[^4] About 420,000 people held a standard FIDE rating.[^5]
- **FIDE can revalue every number it has ever issued, overnight.** On March 1, 2024 it added up to 400 points to roughly 350,000 players — the bottom 85% of the list — under the formula 0.40 × (2000 − rating), and raised the rating floor from 1000 to 1400.[^5]

## What FIDE actually sells

Arpad Elo was a Marquette physics professor and chess master who replaced the ad-hoc Harkness system with a statistical model of expected score. The US Chess Federation adopted it in 1960; FIDE adopted it in 1970 and published its first international list in 1971. Until the mid-1980s Elo computed the ratings himself.[^6]

What FIDE bought in 1970 was not a formula. Formulas are free — Elo's is four lines of arithmetic, Glicko and Glicko-2 are published and unencumbered, and any competent engineer can implement either in an afternoon. What FIDE bought was the exclusive right to decide which games count. That right is what a title is made of. A grandmaster norm requires performance against a specified mix of titled opponents in a FIDE-rated event with a FIDE-licensed arbiter, and the resulting title is awarded for life and recognised in every country. The rating is the coursework; the title is the licence. This is precisely the structure of [the bachelor's degree](bachelors-degree.md), where the lectures are free and the accreditation is not.

The fee schedule follows from that. FIDE prices the rating like a utility because the rating is a customer-acquisition channel for the title and the event. A national federation pays €0.30 per game so its tournament is rated, so its players accumulate norms, so a handful become titled, so the federation retains members and FIDE sells more entries into its own championship cycle. Everything upstream is priced near marginal cost.

FIDE's president made the logic explicit when extending the waiver: "While rating fees are a source of revenue for FIDE, this waiver is widely welcomed by national federations as it supports both organizers and players. We believe this is not the right time to impose additional financial burdens. Instead, federations can redirect these funds toward increased prize pools and greater opportunities for players."[^2] Read commercially, that is a decision to trade €2.2 million of fee revenue for a denser match graph, because the graph is what makes the title credible and the events worth entering.

## The March 2024 revaluation showed who owns the number

For years the bottom of the FIDE list had been deflating: the floor had been walked down from 1800 in 2001 to 1000 by August 2012, and each cohort of underrated newcomers pulled points out of the pool. On March 1, 2024 FIDE fixed it by decree. Roughly 350,000 players — everyone below 2000 — received an increase of 0.40 × (2000 − rating), so a 1000-rated player gained 400 points and a 1950-rated player gained 20. The floor moved to 1400. About 70,000 players above 2000 were untouched.[^5]

Statistician Jeff Sonas described the aim as compressing the 1400–2000 band "without affecting the order of the players, only spacing them." International arbiter Otto Milvang objected that a one-time adjustment does not address the mechanism that produced the deflation.[^5]

Both are second-order points. The first-order fact is that a single committee restated the recorded ability of 85% of the rated population by up to forty percent of a rating band, and nobody left. No private rating survives that. If chess.com added 400 points to every account below 2000, the number would be understood as arbitrary and the product would lose the only property users care about — that yesterday's number and today's are comparable. FIDE could do it because its ratings are not the product. The title is, and titles were untouched.

The transferable observation: **a rating you can unilaterally rewrite is not a measurement, it is an administrative instrument, and it is only tolerable when the customer is buying something else.**

## Two customers, two buying motions

**The federation, buying rated status by the game.** This is the volume business and it is unglamorous. The buyer is an organiser, not a player. What the organiser purchases is the guarantee that the games played in their hall will move a number that FIDE recognises, which is what makes players travel to the hall. The price is cents per game, the contract is annual, and FIDE's leverage is that no substitute exists — a tournament that is not FIDE-rated produces no norms. The weakness is equally structural: at €0.30 a game, the entire category grossed €1.47 million across every federation on earth in 2024, less than FIDE spent on staff salaries.[^1]

**Freestyle Chess, buying the words.** The more instructive case is the challenger. Freestyle Chess — Chess960 played under a Grand Slam Tour format, with Magnus Carlsen both competing and invested — spent 2025 in public conflict with FIDE, substantially over whether an event outside FIDE's cycle may be called a World Championship. In February 2026 the dispute resolved in the only direction the economics allowed. The event ran February 13–15 at Weissenhaus under the name **FIDE** Freestyle Chess World Championship, contested by the top six from the Freestyle Chess Grand Slam Tour — Carlsen, Aronian, Caruana, Keymer, Sindarov and Erigaisi — and won by Carlsen over Caruana 2.5–1.5, his twenty-first world title. FIDE published the result on its own site.[^7]

Note what each side supplied. Freestyle Chess supplied the format, the venue, the money, the broadcast and the players, including the best one alive. FIDE supplied the adjective. *That the naming right was the whole of FIDE's contribution is an inference from the structure of the deal; neither party has published its commercial terms.* The Nakamura episode reinforces how thin the underlying property is: he had been invited to the first leg of a year-long 2026 tour on 2025 terms, then told days before the announcement that there would be no year-long tour, only a three-day rapid event carrying the championship name.[^8]

A competitor with a nine-figure backer, the world's strongest player and a genuinely better product for the format still ended up renting the one asset FIDE holds. That is what an intact monopoly on nomenclature looks like — and it is worth noting that it is a monopoly on nomenclature, not on chess.

## The comparator is Lichess, not chess.com

The instinct is to compare FIDE with chess.com. The better comparator is Lichess, because it isolates the variable. Lichess runs Glicko-2, the same family of estimator, over a comparable volume of games; it is AGPL-licensed, adless, free in every feature, operated as a nonprofit on donations, with roughly seven million active players.[^9] It has given away the artifact, the code and the gate. It is beloved, it is technically excellent, and it has no revenue model beyond charity — because it holds nothing back that anyone must pay to pass.

Chess.com holds back nothing about the rating either; its Glicko number is free and public. What it holds is the liquidity. Twenty-five million games a day means an opponent within fifty points at any hour in any time control, and that is the good being sold. The paid tier is analysis, lessons and courses — Chessable and Courses added 527,000 new users in Q1 2026, up 268% year on year.[^4] The rating is the routing key for the marketplace, priced at zero, exactly as FIDE prices it.

Three organisations, three different things withheld: FIDE withholds the title, chess.com withholds nothing but owns the order flow, Lichess withholds nothing and owns nothing. Revenue tracks the withholding, with one exception, and the exception is the point — chess.com proves you can substitute scale for a gate, if you get there first. That is the [Supabase](supabase.md) position applied to a rating.

## Did it work

FIDE's own numbers say: partly, and with a widening gap between plan and outcome.

Source: FIDE Verification Commission report on the 2024 financial statements.[^1]

Income more than quintupled from 2018 to 2023, almost entirely because FIDE recovered the rights to run its own major events from World Chess/Agon in 2021. It then fell 16% in 2024 and the year closed with a deficit of €695,410. Donations, which were 40% of income in 2019, were about 1% in 2024. FIDE budgeted €3 million of general sponsorship for 2024 and booked €166,000 — a five percent realisation rate — while the auditors' own commission warned that "FIDE cannot spend money it does not receive," that staff costs are contractual and hard to reverse, and that the shortfall therefore lands on the discretionary Development Fund.[^1]

The decisive undisclosed metric is the renewal economics of the event sponsorships that now carry eighty percent of the organisation. FIDE publishes event income net, so a reader cannot see gross sponsorship, gross costs, or how much of the 2023 peak was one large contract. Until that is disclosed, "FIDE is financially healthy" is not a claim the published statements support in either direction.

## Founder playbook

1. **Price the number at zero and find the thing it qualifies people for.** FIDE waived €2.2 million of rating fees to grow the graph that makes its titles credible. *Test:* if you deleted your rating's price tomorrow, what revenue line would grow within four quarters? If the answer is none, you do not have a ratings business, you have a ratings feature.
2. **Decide early whether you are selling the gate or the liquidity — they need opposite organisations.** A gate business needs arbiters, regulations, appeals and a title register, and can survive with 420,000 users. A liquidity business needs matchmaking, uptime and mobile installs, and dies below critical mass. *Test:* which of the two would break first if your engineering headcount halved?
3. **Never revalue historical scores unless the score is not the product.** FIDE moved 350,000 ratings by up to 400 points and lost nobody, because titles were untouched. *Test:* can you name the artifact your users would still hold if every rating in your database were rewritten tonight? If there is no such artifact, treat historical comparability as a hard constraint.
4. **Expect the challenger to rent your name rather than replace it — and price accordingly.** Freestyle Chess brought the format, the venue, the money and Carlsen, and the 2026 event still carries FIDE's name. *Test:* when a well-funded competitor launches, do they need a word you own? If yes, that word is your product; if no, your moat is switching cost and it is depreciating.
5. **Publish gross, not net, if you want the credential to be trusted.** FIDE reports event income net and its own verification commission cannot assess sustainability from it. *Test:* can an outsider reconstruct your revenue concentration from what you publish? A credential issuer whose finances are opaque is one bad sponsorship cycle from a legitimacy problem.
6. **Watch for the moment your fee revenue stops covering the cost of collecting it.** €1.47 million from every federation on earth is a rounding error against a €16.4 million cost base. *Test:* what is the fully loaded cost of your billing, invoicing and collections for your smallest revenue line?

The durable rule: **a rating is worth what its issuer can refuse to grant.** FIDE can refuse a title, so it can afford to give away the rating. Chess.com can refuse nothing and had to win on scale instead. Lichess refuses nothing and owns nothing, which is a legitimate choice and not a business. Before building a scoring product, decide which of the three you intend to be, because the three require different companies.
