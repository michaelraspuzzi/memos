Closing the Source

Coursera spent thirteen years distributing other institutions' credentials. In 2026 it started charging them rent.

Founder audience: builders of marketplaces that intermediate a credential someone else issues. Coursera's founding bet was that the accredited degree was the valuable end of the education stack and that the company that distributed it would capture a share. The degree line peaked at 8.3% of revenue, carried a 100% gross margin because Coursera did no work in it, was retired as a reporting segment in the first quarter under a new CEO, and never recovered its disclosure. In its place Coursera repriced its suppliers twice — to engagement-based revenue share in 2025, to an outright platform fee in 2026 — and then paid stock for Udemy's 95,000 unaccredited instructors. The lesson is not that credentials do not matter. It is that a distributor of someone else's credential owns the audience and nothing else, and eventually monetizes the only asset it has by taxing the issuer.

The position in five facts

  • The credential line was always the smallest line. Coursera's Degrees segment produced $57.7 million of 2024's $694.7 million in revenue, or 8.3%, against $398.1 million from Consumer and $238.9 million from Enterprise.1 It ran at a 100% gross margin, because Coursera paid no content cost against it — the revenue was the service fee on tuition collected by the university.2
  • Degrees reached 26,700 students against 168 million registered learners. That is one degree student per 6,300 registrations, at the end of 2024, after twelve years of building the funnel.2
  • The disclosure ended in the new CEO's first quarter. Greg Hart became CEO on February 3, 2025.3 In the Q1 2025 release Coursera folded Degrees into Consumer and stopped publishing the student count, stating that the metric "does not provide a meaningful indication for Consumer segment performance." That quarter Degrees revenue was $15.5 million, up 5%.4
  • Coursera has now repriced its content partners twice in two years. In 2025 it moved creators from enrollment-based to engagement-based revenue share. In 2026 it "introduced a platform fee charged to our content creators" on consumer subscriptions, courses and enterprise sales, and disclosed in the same paragraph: "We have experienced opposition to our content fee terms, and we anticipate similar challenges in the future."5
  • The answer to slowing growth was an all-stock merger with the least credentialed catalog available. Coursera closed its combination with Udemy on May 11, 2026 at 0.8 Coursera shares per Udemy share, targeting $115 million of run-rate cost synergies within 24 months.6 Q2 2026 revenue was $298.6 million, up 60% as reported and down 1% normalized for the merger.7

What Coursera actually sells, and to whom

Coursera was launched in 2012 by Andrew Ng and Daphne Koller.7 Strip the mission statement and there are three businesses inside it, and they have almost nothing in common except a login.

Consumer is a subscription. Coursera Plus gives a learner access to more than 13,500 items for an annual or monthly fee, and Coursera pays the content creator a share of net revenue attributable to their content.5 The economics are those of a streaming service: acquire cheaply, retain, and hold the content cost below the subscription price. In 2025, over 69% of cash receipts from projects, courses, specializations and certificates came from learners who were already registered before the period began, which is the number that makes the marketing spend work.5

Enterprise is seats. A company, campus or government buys licences, and Coursera books it as subscription revenue with one-to-three-year terms. Gross margin is the best in the business at 79% in Q2 2026.7

Degrees was never a product Coursera made. A university partner enrolls the student, sets the tuition, collects the tuition, awards the degree, and pays Coursera a percentage as a service fee.1 Coursera hosts and markets. That is why the segment showed 100% gross margin — there is no cost of goods when you are the affiliate.

That last structure is the whole memo. In the two segments where Coursera does real work it earns 65–79% margins on volume it controls. In the segment attached to the actual credential it earned a 100% margin on volume it did not control, from a partner who could leave, under a regulatory permission it does not own: US degree revenue sharing depends on the Department of Education's 2011 "bundled services exception" to the incentive compensation rule, and Coursera says plainly that its "current degrees business model relies heavily" on it. A 2024 Minnesota law already restricts public universities from entering such arrangements.5

A credential distributor with no cost of goods sounds like a wonderful business until you notice that no cost of goods means no contribution, and no contribution means no leverage over the party that issues the credential.

The turning point was not a decision. It was a disclosure.

Read the quarters in order.

Degrees students, in thousands, ran 18.1, 19.1, 20.4, 22.0 through 2023 and 22.2, 22.6, 26.4, 26.7 through 2024 — growth of roughly 22% a year off a base small enough that the entire program would fit in one large state university.1 Degrees revenue grew from $51.0 million in 2023 to $57.7 million in 2024, then $15.5 million in Q1 2025, up 5% year on year.14 Deceleration, not collapse.

Then, in his first reported quarter, the incoming CEO merged the segment into Consumer and removed the metric. Coursera's stated reason — that degree student counts do not meaningfully indicate Consumer segment performance — is circular: the counts stopped being meaningful because the segment they measured had been abolished. The reclassification moved $15.5 million of 100%-margin revenue into Consumer, which is precisely why Consumer's reported gross margin jumped from 55.7% to 61.6% on the new basis in the same release.4

Nothing here is improper. Segment reporting follows how management runs the business, and Hart plainly runs it as one consumer funnel. But a founder should read the sequence for what it is: the company stopped separately measuring the credential at the exact moment the credential stopped growing faster than everything else. When you own the reporting boundary, "we simplified" and "we stopped scoring the part that was losing" produce identical disclosures.

Two buyers, two directions of payment

Google, as the content partner who owns the signal. Coursera's entry-level Professional Certificates are authored by employer brands — Amazon, Google, IBM, Meta, Microsoft.5 The Google Data Analytics Certificate carries an American Council on Education credit recommendation of up to 12 college credits.5 This is the best product Coursera sells, and note who supplies the value: the learner is buying Google's assertion, delivered on Coursera's platform, credit-mapped by ACE. Coursera contributes reach and a payment page. It is a superb business while the employer wants the distribution, and it converts to nothing the day the employer decides to host the certificate itself.

The university partner, who now pays twice. Until 2025 the flow ran one way: student pays university, university pays Coursera a share. From 2025 creators are paid on learner engagement rather than enrollment — the streaming-royalty model, which rewards watch time over completion. From 2026 there is also a platform fee flowing the other way, charged to creators on consumer and enterprise sales.5 Coursera has become both the toll road and the toll. It is the App Store move, executed thirteen years in, and the 10-K's admission of "opposition" is the tell that it was executed from need rather than strength.

The comparator is not Udemy and it is not edX. It is 2U, the pure-play online program manager that bet everything on degree revenue share, bought edX for $800 million in 2021, filed for Chapter 11 on July 25, 2024, and emerged as a private company that September.8 2U is what Coursera's Degrees segment looks like when it is the whole company. Coursera survived the same structural weakness because two-thirds of its revenue was never in that business.

Growth halved from 21% to 9% and stayed there for two years. The merger arithmetic is the more useful test. Q2 2026 revenue of $298.6 million was up 60% as reported and down 1% normalized for the combination; Enterprise was up 118% reported and 3% normalized; Consumer was up 29% reported and down 5% normalized.7 The quarter carried $79.8 million of merger, integration and restructuring costs and produced a net loss of $80.4 million, against $42.7 million of adjusted EBITDA.7

Underneath, two counters move in opposite directions. Enterprise customers, on the combined company's recast basis, have gone 12,387, 12,325, 12,338, 12,312, 12,214, 12,107 over six quarters — down 2% year on year and lower in five of the six — with net retention sliding from 95% to 91%. Paid consumer subscribers have gone the other way, 1.079 million to 1.655 million over the same six quarters.7 Coursera is trading enterprise accounts for consumer subscriptions, which is a trade toward lower price points and away from the segment with 79% margins.

The ratio that frames everything: more than 300 million registered learners and 1.655 million paid subscribers is a 0.55% conversion.7 Coursera's real product is a very large free audience, monetized at the rate of a media property. Its own 2026 Micro-Credentials Impact Report reports that 92% of employers say entry-level hires with micro-credentials perform better in their first year — a company-commissioned survey of 3,500 respondents, and worth reading as marketing rather than evidence.7

The decisive undisclosed metric is now degree revenue and degree student count. Coursera still lists 50-plus degrees as of December 31, 2025 and still books tuition-share revenue inside Consumer.5 Whether that line is growing, flat or shrinking is no longer knowable from the outside, and the company chose that.

Founder playbook

  1. If you distribute someone else's credential, price the distribution, not the credential. Coursera's degree segment showed 100% gross margin, which reads as free money and is actually a warning: no cost of goods means no operating contribution and no leverage. Test: if your largest supplier left tomorrow, what work would you still be doing? If the answer is "marketing," you are an affiliate, and your take rate is a negotiation you will lose.
  2. Never build a revenue line on a regulatory exception you did not obtain. Coursera's US degree economics rest on a 2011 Department of Education guidance letter, and one state has already legislated against the arrangement.5 Test: name the specific document that makes your revenue legal, and the single office that could withdraw it. If you cannot, you do not know your risk.
  3. Watch for the quarter a company stops reporting a segment. It usually arrives with a new CEO and the word "simplified." Test: when you retire a metric, publish the last twelve quarters of it restated. If you are unwilling to, you are not simplifying.
  4. Repricing your supply side is a confession about your demand side. Coursera changed creator revenue share to engagement in 2025, added a platform fee in 2026, and disclosed opposition to it.5 Aggregators tax suppliers when they can no longer grow the top line. Test: is your take-rate increase funded by new value you delivered to the supplier this year, or by the fact that they cannot leave?
  5. Buy catalog for coverage, not for credibility. The Udemy combination added roughly 95,000 creators and 315,000 courses and made normalized revenue slightly negative.7 Scale of catalog and quality of signal are separate assets and merging them dilutes the second. Test: after your acquisition, does the median item in your catalog raise or lower what a buyer assumes about a random item?
  6. Measure conversion against registrations, not against actives. 1.655 million paid on 300 million registered is 0.55%. Test: if your conversion rate has three leading zeros, your growth plan is a media plan, and you should staff and value the company accordingly.

The durable rule: an intermediary cannot inherit the trust of the thing it intermediates. Coursera made university credentials available to 300 million people and never made a Coursera credential mean anything on its own, which is why the credential line stayed small, then got folded away, and why the company now has to charge its partners for shelf space. If you are building the distribution layer under someone else's gate, assume the gate keeps its value and you keep the traffic — and build the business that traffic can actually support. Compare the bachelor's degree, where the lectures are free and the licensure is not, and chess ratings, where the number is given away and the title is sold.
